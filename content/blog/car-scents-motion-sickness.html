Best Car Scents for Motion Sickness: Peppermint, Ginger, and Calming Blends

Three scent families come up again and again in both research and product design, and each one fits a slightly different kind of queasiness.

Peppermint is the closest thing to a go-to. Simulator research has linked peppermint to reduced motion-sickness symptoms alongside improved alertness, which makes it a solid pick for sudden, mild nausea that hits partway through a drive. It’s sharp enough to notice immediately, and that matters: pleasant scents tend to help mostly when people consciously perceive them, so a barely-there peppermint hanger is doing real work, not just sitting there.

Ginger has a longer history as an antiemetic, and it tends to suit persistent, stomach-centered nausea better than the sudden kind. If someone in the car gets queasy from the first mile and stays that way, ginger is worth trying before peppermint.

Bergamot and lavender blends work differently. They’re calming rather than stimulating, which helps when anxiety about the drive itself is part of the problem. The research on these two is more mixed than peppermint or ginger, so treat them as a good option for anxious passengers, not a universal fix.

On delivery: slow-release hangers that release scent steadily over weeks beat a strong aerosol burst almost every time, because a constant low dose keeps the scent noticeable without overwhelming anyone. What Does the Research Say About Scent and Nausea?

The evidence isn’t unanimous, but it does point in a consistent direction. A PubMed-indexed review covering multiple clinical and simulator trials found peppermint and ginger scents associated with reduced nausea and motion-sickness symptoms, though results shift depending on the study design and how the scent was delivered. A separate comparative simulator experiment tested peppermint, ginger, and ginger-lavender combinations against controls and found measurable drops in motion-sickness indicators using both subjective rating scales and physiological tracking.

That physiological tracking matters. Researchers lean on tools like the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ), galvanic skin response (GSR), and EEG readings because “I feel less sick” is hard to verify on its own. When pleasant odors reduced visually induced motion sickness in lab settings, the effect only showed up in participants who consciously noticed the smell. Unnoticed scents did little. That’s the strongest argument against dousing a car in fragrance: a low, perceptible dose outperforms an overpowering one.

Not every review agrees on magnitude. Some broader syntheses of aromatherapy research describe the overall evidence as mixed, varying by scent, delivery method, and individual sensitivity. That’s a reason to treat scent as a low-risk adjunct, not a guaranteed cure. On the safety side, the EPA’s guidance on volatile organic compounds is a good reason to favor slow-release, IFRA-compliant formulations over high-concentration sprays that spike VOC levels in a closed cabin.

How Do You Use Car Scents Safely?

Getting the intensity and placement right prevents a good idea from backfiring.

  1. Start with the weakest setting or the mildest hanger you own, and get a thumbs-up from every passenger before a long drive.
  2. Hang the scent at head height, but off to the side rather than directly under anyone’s nose, and check where the vents are blowing.
  3. On longer trips, give the cabin scent-free breaks rather than running an intense source the whole way.
  4. Favor 4 to 6 week slow-release hangers over high-concentration cartridges or aerosol sprays, which peak hard and fade fast.
  5. Check the label for IFRA compliance or clear ingredient disclosure, and crack a window regularly to keep VOC levels down, especially in a smaller cabin.

Pro Tip: Run a short test before committing to a road trip. Ten to fifteen minutes in a ventilated, parked car at the lowest scent intensity, with the passenger reporting any change, tells you more than guessing ever will. Pull the scent immediately if anything worsens.

Can a Car Scent Make Motion Sickness Worse?

Yes, and it usually comes down to one of a few things. Some people are just more sensitive to smell, and an unfamiliar or synthetic-leaning fragrance can trigger the same aversion response as spoiled food. Choosing familiar, food-associated notes (think mint or ginger over something exotic) tends to sit better with sensitive noses, and this guide to natural, familiar car scents walks through picking gentler options.

New-car smell is its own problem. It’s largely off-gassing VOCs, and that chemical load can spike nausea before you’ve added any fragrance at all. Ventilate aggressively and skip strong synthetic air fresheners on top of it. Pregnancy, certain medications, and chronic conditions can also change how someone reacts to concentrated aromatherapy, so it’s worth checking with a doctor before leaning on active scent therapy in those cases. If symptoms do worsen, poison-control guidance on air fresheners recommends opening windows, removing the scent source, and switching to non-scent measures right away.

Quick Fixes That Work Alongside Scent

A few low-effort moves pair well with any scent strategy, or work on their own when scent isn’t the answer:

  • Sit in the front seat if possible, face forward, and fix your eyes on the horizon rather than a phone or book.
  • Crack a window for airflow, since stale, warm air makes nausea worse regardless of fragrance.
  • Chew gum or suck on a peppermint lozenge, which gives you an oral peppermint dose that complements a scent hanger; peppermint-flavored chewing gum is a simple option to keep in the glove box.
  • Sip water, eat something light, and try slow, controlled breathing before reaching for an over-the-counter antiemetic.

What is the best car scent for motion sickness?

Peppermint and ginger have the strongest research backing among scent options for motion-nausea relief, according to a PubMed-indexed review of clinical and simulator trials. Why do car scents sometimes make me nauseous instead of helping?

Strong or unfamiliar fragrances can trigger the same aversion reflex as spoiled food, especially in people who are already sensitive to smell. High-concentration sprays and cartridges also push VOC levels up in a closed cabin, and the EPA links elevated VOC exposure to worse indoor air quality, which can compound queasiness rather than ease it.

What scent is good for motion sickness if I’m prone to anxiety in the car?

Bergamot and lavender blends tend to work better for anxiety-linked nausea than sharper scents like peppermint, since they’re calming rather than stimulating. A slow-release option like Heavens delivers that calmer profile at a low, steady intensity rather than an intense burst.

Can new-car smell cause or worsen motion sickness?

Yes. New-car smell comes largely from off-gassing volatile organic compounds, and that chemical load can spike nausea on its own, before any air freshener is added. Ventilating the cabin and avoiding additional strong synthetic scents on top of that new-car VOC load helps keep symptoms manageable.
