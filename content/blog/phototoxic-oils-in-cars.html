What phototoxicity is and which oils commonly cause it

Phototoxicity happens when certain chemical compounds in an oil react with ultraviolet light on skin, producing a reaction that looks like an exaggerated sunburn. The culprits are usually furanocoumarins, most notably bergapten, which absorb UV energy and trigger damage in exposed skin cells.

The Tisserand Institute explains that this reactivity is common in several cold-pressed citrus oils:

  • Bergamot, the most studied and highest-risk of the group.
  • Lemon, lime, and grapefruit, all cold-pressed varieties.
  • Bitter orange, along with a handful of non-citrus oils used occasionally in fragrance blends.

Extraction method changes the risk profile substantially. Cold-pressing citrus peel keeps the furanocoumarins intact, while steam distillation leaves most of them behind, and FCF processing removes bergapten deliberately from the finished oil.

IFRA and EU guidance caps bergapten at 15 parts per million in finished products meant for sun-exposed skin, even though raw bergamot oil naturally contains significantly higher amounts, typically between 0.11 and 0.33 percent. That gap between raw and finished-product limits explains why FCF bergamot exists as a standard safer substitute in personal care and fragrance industries.

How phototoxic reactions present and exposure pathways relevant to cars

A phototoxic reaction typically starts as redness and swelling that mimics sunburn, often within hours of sun exposure following skin contact. In more severe cases, it progresses to blistering, and once it heals, it can leave behind patches of hyperpigmentation that last for months.

Inside a car, exposure does not require deliberate application. Common pathways include:

  1. Direct contact from spraying a diluted oil on skin or clothing before driving.
  2. Residue transferred from hands after opening or refilling a bottle.
  3. Oil pooling on upholstery or car seats from a leaking diffuser or hanger.
  4. Concentrated droplets landing on skin from a cracked cartridge or overfilled reservoir.

The distinction between wash-off and leave-on exposure matters more than most people assume. A quick rinse removes most of the phototoxic load, but oil left on skin, especially under a car’s windows where UV penetrates freely, keeps reacting for as long as it sits there. Research on citrus oil UV absorption ties this reactivity to absorption around 320 nanometers, a wavelength that passes through most windshield and side glass. If skin contact happens, avoiding direct sun for 12 to 48 hours afterward reduces the odds of a visible reaction.

Risks to car interiors and material compatibility

Phototoxic oils are not just a skin concern. The same cold-pressed citrus oils that carry furanocoumarins are also chemically aggressive toward plastics, vinyl, and leather, particularly in the heat a parked car generates under direct sun.

  • Undiluted citrus oil can soften plastic trim and cause plasticizers to leach out, leaving a tacky residue.
  • Leather and vinyl can stain permanently where concentrated oil sits for hours.
  • High cabin temperatures accelerate every one of these reactions compared to room-temperature exposure.
  • UV exposure through glass adds a second stressor that speeds up oil breakdown and surface damage together.

Manufacturers who build fragrances specifically for vehicles typically run heat-soak testing and fogging or panel tests, exposing materials to sustained high temperatures to confirm the formula will not phase-separate, stain, or off-gas in ways that damage trim. Pro Tip: Check the underside of any hanger or vent clip monthly. Tackiness or a faint ring on the plastic means the oil is already migrating into the material.

Safer choices and practical rules for using scented oils in cars

The simplest fix is substitution: swap cold-pressed citrus for FCF or steam-distilled versions, or choose a fragrance oil built for automotive use rather than a raw essential oil repurposed from skincare.

  • Avoid cold-pressed bergamot, lemon, lime, or grapefruit in anything that could contact skin or pool on fabric.
  • Choose FCF bergamot, steam-distilled citrus, or an automotive-grade fragrance oil formulated for slow release.
  • Use sealed cartridges or hanger formats rather than open dishes or direct application to vents.
  • Keep oils away from upholstery seams, where pooling is hardest to spot and clean.

A few practical rules round this out:

  1. Never combine two phototoxic citrus oils at their individual maximum concentrations, since the risk compounds.
  2. Favor a single, verified safe oil over a blend of unknowns.
  3. Skip fragrance entirely before sunbathing, tanning, or any activity involving prolonged direct sun on bare skin.

The difference between a fragrance oil and an essential oil matters here too, and it is worth understanding before choosing between them for a car hanger.

Practical first aid and mitigation if you suspect exposure

If oil has touched skin and you are not sure how photoactive it was, treat it like a real exposure rather than waiting to see what happens.

  1. Wash the area thoroughly with soap and water as soon as possible.
  2. Cover the skin with clothing or a bandage to block sunlight.
  3. Avoid direct sun exposure for 12 to 48 hours, matching the window most reactions develop in.
  4. Apply sunscreen to the area as a secondary precaution, not a substitute for covering it.
  5. Photograph the product label and note the batch or lot number in case you need to report it.

Pro Tip: Keep the original bottle or packaging until any reaction has fully resolved. It is the fastest way to identify the exact oil if a doctor needs to know.

Most reactions resolve with basic care, but large blisters, severe pain, or any sign of a systemic reaction such as fever or widespread rash warrant a same-day visit to a doctor rather than waiting it out.

An automotive design perspective on safer car scents

Working in car fragrance design means thinking about UV and heat as design constraints, not afterthoughts. A cabin left in direct sun routinely gets hotter than a bathroom shelf ever will, and it does it every single day the car is parked outside.

Automotive-grade slow-release formats reduce risk on two fronts at once. A sealed hanger limits how much oil is ever exposed to skin or upholstery at one time, and a formula tested for heat stability is far less likely to break down into something that stains or off-gases. Some automotive hangers are built around this logic, using eco-friendly ingredients in a slow-release design intended to evolve over four to six weeks rather than fading or pooling early. Anyone buying a car fragrance, from any brand, should look for a clear statement on FCF or steam-distilled citrus content and read the use instructions before installing it.

FAQ

Which oils are phototoxic?

Cold-pressed bergamot, lemon, lime, grapefruit, and bitter orange carry the main phototoxic risk because they retain furanocoumarins like bergapten. Steam-distilled or FCF versions of these same oils have most or all of that compound removed.

Are any essential oils safe for cars?

FCF bergamot and steam-distilled citrus oils are considered low risk for phototoxicity since the reactive compounds have been removed or were never extracted. Automotive-grade fragrance oils designed for slow-release hangers, like those Voidescent uses, also avoid the raw, high-concentration exposure that causes problems.

What does phototoxicity look like?

It typically starts as redness and swelling resembling an intense sunburn, appearing on skin that had oil contact before sun exposure. Severe cases can blister, and healed skin often shows lasting hyperpigmentation.

Which essential oils are not photosensitive?

Steam-distilled citrus oils and FCF (bergapten-free) versions of bergamot are formulated specifically to avoid the UV-reactive compounds found in cold-pressed citrus. Many non-citrus essential oils also fall outside this risk category, though checking the specific oil’s furanocoumarin content is the reliable way to confirm it.
